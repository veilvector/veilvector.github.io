How Does the Internet Actually Work?

You type a website address, press Enter, and a page appears. But between those two moments, your request has to find its way across a world of connected networks.

01 / The familiar part

One address. One click. A lot happening.

Let's try something simple.

Open your browser, type a website address, and press Enter.

A request begins here
https://example.com

A moment later, the page appears. It feels almost instant.

But your computer didn't magically know where that website was. It needed to work out where to send the request, get it across several networks, and receive a response from the right server.

Think about it

If the Internet connects millions of devices, how does your computer know where to go?

02 / Follow the request

Let's trace its journey

Imagine visiting a website from your laptop. Your browser starts the process, but the request doesn't jump straight to its destination. It moves through connected systems along the way.

01 Your device

And the journey doesn't end at the server. The response must travel back to your device, where your browser uses the returned resources to display the page.

03 / Connected networks

The Internet isn't one giant computer

Your laptop might connect through home Wi-Fi. Your phone might use a mobile network. A college computer might use the institution's own network.

These local networks can communicate with other networks through equipment and services that help move data between them.

Beyond your own connection are internet service providers, routers, data centres, servers, and many other interconnected systems.

The Internet is a network of networks.

No single machine contains the entire Internet. Its connectivity comes from many separate networks working together.

04 / Finding a destination

How does the traffic know where to go?

Devices need addressing information to communicate across networks. One important part of that system is the IP address.

An IP address helps identify a network destination so traffic can be routed toward it. You don't need to learn every detail yet; we'll explore IP addresses more closely in the next article.

A small clue

When you visit a website, do you usually type its IP address?

Usually, no. You type a name that's easier for people to remember.

05 / Names and numbers

Meet DNS

Imagine having to memorise the numerical address of every website you wanted to visit. That would get tiring very quickly.

Instead, we use domain names, such as example.com.

Your device needs the information required to locate the destination associated with that name. This is where DNS — the Domain Name System — comes in.

“I know the name. Where can I find it?”

DNS helps resolve domain names into records, often including IP addresses that a client can use to continue connecting. The exact process can vary depending on cached information and the network configuration.

Once the browser has the information it needs, the connection process can continue.

06 / Across the Internet

Routers help traffic find its way

Your request doesn't necessarily travel in a straight line from your device to the server.

Routers connect networks and forward packets toward their destinations. Each router uses routing information to decide where to send traffic next.

Your traffic may pass through several routers before reaching the destination network. The exact route can change depending on network conditions and routing decisions.

The Internet hides enormous complexity behind a very simple experience.
07 / The response

What happens when the request arrives?

Somewhere at the other end, a web server is ready to handle incoming requests. Your browser asks for a resource, and the server processes that request.

If everything works as expected, the server sends a response. That response might include HTML, images, stylesheets, JavaScript, or other resources used to build the page.

REQUEST

Your browser asks the server for a resource.

PROCESS

The server handles the request according to the application and its rules.

RESPONSE

The server returns a result, which may include content or an error.

DISPLAY

Your browser interprets the returned resources and renders the page.

That is why a website is more than a picture stored somewhere. It is the result of devices, networks, protocols, servers, and browsers exchanging information.

08 / The security connection

Now look at the journey like a cybersecurity investigator

Understanding how the Internet works gives us more than networking knowledge. It helps us ask better security questions.

  • 01
    Is the connection protected against someone reading or altering the traffic?
  • 02
    Did the domain resolve to an expected destination?
  • 03
    Is the server legitimate, or could it be impersonating another service?
  • 04
    Does the server have a vulnerability that could be exploited?
  • 05
    What can network logs reveal about the connection and its timing?

These questions connect networking to traffic analysis, secure communications, incident investigation, and understanding how attacks move through systems.

The goal isn't to memorise every component at once. It's to understand the journey well enough to recognise when something about it looks unusual.

The takeaway

A website visit is a conversation

When you visit a website, your device uses network information to locate a destination, sends a request across connected networks, receives a response, and turns the returned resources into the page you see.

Every step leaves us with another clue about how digital systems communicate — and how they can be protected.
