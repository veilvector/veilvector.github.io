How Cyber Attacks Actually Happen

A cyber attack rarely tells its whole story in one event. To understand what happened, defenders connect small clues into a sequence—and work out where the attack could be stopped.

Guide 015 · Beginner Path
01 — Follow the clues

An attack is a sequence, not a single moment

Imagine a company notices that an employee's account has behaved strangely. A login looks unfamiliar, a new process appears, and a file is suddenly changed.

Each event matters, but none tells the entire story alone. The challenge is to understand what happened first, what followed, and whether the events are connected.

Many attacks can be understood through a simplified journey. Real incidents may skip stages, repeat them, or follow a different order.

A simplified attack journey

01

Initial access

An attacker gains a way into an account or system.

→
02

Execution

Code or a command runs on a device.

→
03

This is a learning model, not a checklist every attack follows.

02 — First clue

Initial access: how does it begin?

An attacker needs some way to reach a target. One familiar route is phishing: a message tricks someone into opening a malicious attachment, visiting a harmful site, or sharing their credentials.

But phishing is only one possibility. Attackers may also use stolen passwords, exposed services, vulnerable software, or malicious files.

Route 01

Phishing

A deceptive message persuades someone to take an unsafe action.

Route 02

Stolen credentials

A login uses credentials that were exposed or obtained by an attacker.

Route 03

Exposed or vulnerable service

A service reachable over a network has a weakness that can be exploited.

For a defender, the useful question is not just "Was there a suspicious email?" It is also "Was there a login, process, or network event afterwards that helps explain what happened?"

Investigator's question: What evidence shows how access was first obtained?

03 — Second clue

Execution: something runs

Getting access does not always mean an attacker can immediately achieve their goal. They may need a command or program to run on the affected device.

That activity can leave evidence: a new process, a command line, a parent process, a timestamp, or a connection to another system.

Read the process evidence

Process

Which program or command ran?

Parent process

What started it? Was that behaviour expected?

User

Which account was active when it ran?

Time and connections

When did it run, and did it communicate over the network?

One suspicious-looking command does not automatically prove an attack. Administrators and legitimate applications run commands too. The surrounding evidence helps establish whether the activity makes sense.

04 — Third clue

Persistence: access that remains

Some attackers try to maintain access after the initial entry. Depending on the system and technique, they might misuse a scheduled task, a startup entry, a service, or another mechanism that runs again later.

These mechanisms also have legitimate uses. A scheduled task or startup program is not malicious just because it exists. Investigators look at who created it, what it runs, when it appeared, and whether it matches normal system activity.

Look for change, not just presence. An unfamiliar entry that appeared around the time of a suspicious login may deserve closer investigation.

05 — Fourth clue

Discovery: learning the environment

Once inside, an attacker may look around to understand the environment. They may examine users, running processes, network connections, accessible files, or available systems.

Some of these actions are ordinary administrative work. What matters is whether the activity is expected for that account, device, and time.

Users and accounts

Which accounts exist, and what access might they have?

Processes and systems

Which applications or services are running?

Network and files

Which systems can be reached, and what data is available?

Defenders compare these observations with normal behaviour. An unusual command from an account that rarely performs administration may be more significant than the same command on a system-management server.

06 — Fifth clue

Impact: what changed?

Impact is the effect an attack has on people, data, or systems. It might involve stolen information, disrupted services, encrypted files, altered records, or misuse of an account.

Not every attack reaches this stage. A defender may detect suspicious activity early enough to interrupt it before the intended impact occurs.

Possible impact

Data exposure

Information may have been accessed or transferred without permission.

Service disruption

A system or application may become unavailable or unreliable.

Unauthorised changes

Files, configurations, records, or account settings may be altered.

Important: suspicious activity can indicate an attempt, but it does not by itself prove that data was stolen or damage occurred. Look for evidence of the actual outcome.

07 — Connect the evidence

Build a timeline, not a guess

Imagine a defender reviewing several events from the same device. Each one may have an innocent explanation, but together they could reveal a pattern worth investigating.

An illustrative investigation timeline

Event 01

Unexpected message

An employee receives an unusual email.

Event 02

Unfamiliar login

An account signs in from an unexpected context.

Event 03

New process

A process runs that needs further explanation.

Event 04

Network activity

The device communicates with an unfamiliar destination.

Event 05

File activity

Files are accessed or changed around the same period.

Example only. These events do not prove an attack on their own. Confirm timestamps, device identity, and supporting evidence.

The timeline helps an analyst ask better questions: Did the events happen on the same device? Are the timestamps consistent? Does the process explain the network connection? Is there evidence that files were actually accessed or changed?

This is how investigation moves beyond isolated alerts. Analysts correlate evidence, test possible explanations, and decide whether the activity is malicious, benign, or still uncertain.

08 — Connect what you know

The clues are connected

The networking concepts you have already explored help make sense of attack activity. An IP address identifies a network endpoint, a port points to a service, and DNS helps explain how a domain name relates to an address.

Process behaviour and malware clues help explain what ran on a device. Authentication and authorization help distinguish how someone proved their identity from what they were allowed to do.

The CIA triad gives defenders another way to understand the consequences: confidentiality, integrity, and availability may each be affected by an incident.

THE TAKEAWAY

Cyber attacks often leave a sequence of clues. Initial access, execution, persistence, discovery, and impact are useful concepts for understanding that sequence, even though real incidents do not always follow the same path.

A defender's job is to detect suspicious activity, connect the evidence, investigate alternative explanations, and interrupt or contain an attack when the evidence supports it.
