Authentication vs Authorization

You proved who you are. Does that mean you can access everything? Not quite. One security process verifies your identity; another decides what you're allowed to do.

Beginner's guide · Identity and access
01 — The login

You're inside. But what can you access?

Imagine opening your college ERP portal. You enter your username, type your password, and click Sign In. A moment later, your dashboard appears.

The system has accepted your login. You can now see your timetable, attendance, and marks.

But could you open another student's private records? Change your lecturer's marks? Create a new administrator account?

Probably not. Even though you signed in successfully, the system should limit what you can do.

That brings us to two closely related concepts: authentication and authorization. They work together, but they answer different questions.

02 — First question

Authentication: who are you?

Authentication is the process of verifying a user's or system's claimed identity.

When you sign in, the system checks evidence that helps establish whether you are the account holder. That evidence might be a password, a security key, or a biometric check.

IDENTITY CHECK
SIGN-IN REQUEST
Verify your identity
The system checks the credentials or other authentication evidence supplied during sign-in.
◇ Authentication

Authentication answers: “Can the system verify the identity being claimed?”

There are three common categories of authentication factors:

Something you know

A password or PIN. The evidence is information you know.

Something you have

A hardware security key or an authenticator device that provides a code or approves a sign-in.

Something you are

A biometric characteristic, such as a fingerprint or facial recognition, supported by the device.

Multi-factor authentication combines factors from different categories. Using two passwords alone does not count as two different factor types.

03 — Second question

Authorization: what are you allowed to do?

Once an identity has been authenticated, the system still needs to decide which resources and actions that identity is permitted to access.

That decision is called authorization.

Allow or deny

Think about the college portal again. Different accounts may have different permissions because their users have different responsibilities.

A student

May view their own attendance, timetable, and results, but should not be able to change official marks.

A lecturer

May enter or update marks for assigned classes, subject to the college's rules and the lecturer's permissions.

An administrator

May manage accounts and system settings within their assigned administrative responsibilities.

All three users can successfully authenticate. Their permissions should still differ.

04 — Connect the clues

One login, two separate decisions

Let's follow a request to view a student's marks.

Sign-in attempt
→
Authentication
Identity verified
→
Authorization check
Permission decision
→
Resource access

First, the system verifies the identity. Then, when the user requests a resource, the system checks whether that identity has permission to perform the requested action.

The precise implementation varies. Authorization can depend on roles, individual permissions, resource ownership, or other rules. A secure system checks the relevant permissions rather than assuming every authenticated user can do everything.

05 — The security problem

What if someone steals a password?

Imagine an attacker obtains a student's password and tries to sign in.

If the system accepts the credentials and no other protection blocks the attempt, the attacker may be authenticated as that account. The system might not know that the person at the keyboard is not the real student.

But authentication does not automatically grant administrator permissions. A properly designed system should still restrict the account to the resources and actions it is authorized to use.

Two controls, two jobs: authentication helps establish identity; authorization limits access. Strong authentication helps protect accounts, while correct authorization helps prevent accounts from accessing resources they shouldn't.

This is also why defenders investigate more than whether a login succeeded. They look at which account was used, what resources were accessed, whether the activity was expected, and whether permissions were appropriate.

06 — Think like an analyst

A sensitive file was opened. Now what?

Suppose a security alert reports that an account accessed a sensitive document. That event alone doesn't prove an attack. A legitimate employee might need the file for work.

A security analyst builds context by asking a few questions.

INVESTIGATION NOTES
Review the access event

Identity: Which account performed the action?

Authentication: How did the session begin?

Resource: Which file or record was accessed?

Authorization: Was this account permitted to access it?

Context: Was the activity expected?

The answers help distinguish legitimate work from possible credential theft, excessive permissions, or unauthorized access. The goal is to establish what happened before deciding how to respond.

07 — One last distinction

Remember these two questions

**Authentication**

Who are you?

+
**Authorization**

What are you allowed to do?

PAUSE FOR A SECOND

A student signs in successfully but tries to change their official marks. Which security process should determine whether that action is permitted?

THE TAKEAWAY

Authentication verifies identity. Authorization determines access. A secure system needs both: it should verify who is signing in and enforce what that identity is allowed to see or do.
